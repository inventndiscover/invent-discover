Then

Dots first, then the line

Before the sun is up, the doorstep is swept and sprinkled with water. Then rice flour, trickled between two fingers and steered by the thumb, sets down a grid of dots, the pulli. Only after that does the line begin, looping around the dots and never through them.

There are many styles. In some, the line joins the dots. In a sikku or kambi kolam it wraps around each dot and knots back on itself, and the most admired ones are a single closed line: start anywhere, pass around every dot, end where you began. Drawing one in a single stroke, without lifting the hand, was a point of pride.

Researchers who study these designs boil the rules down to three. Every dot is enclosed. Lines may cross, but never run along each other. Every line closes: no loose ends.

And then it disappears. Ants, birds and insects eat the flour; feet and wind wear away the rest. The next morning it is drawn again. In Margazhi, the Tamil month that runs from mid-December to mid-January, kolams grow larger and neighbours hold friendly competitions.

A pattern you can write down

A rule strict enough to follow every morning is strict enough to write down. At Madras Christian College in Tambaram, the statistician Gift Siromoney, his wife and colleague Rani Siromoney, and the computer scientist Kamala Krithivasan did exactly that. They recorded kolams stage by stage as they were drawn, then described each one as a sentence in a picture language: a few rewriting rules that turn a small array of symbols into a larger one. Their 1974 paper was called, simply, “Array Grammars and Kolam”.

Once a kolam is a grammar, a computer can draw it. Later researchers named these “Siromoney array grammars”, and in the late 1980s Kamala Krithivasan and Przemysław Prusinkiewicz used L-systems to generate South Indian folk-art patterns.

L-systems began in 1968 with the biologist Aristid Lindenmayer, who used them to describe how plants grow: replace every piece of a drawing with a slightly bigger piece, and repeat. The same trick now grows trees, ferns and branching shapes in computer graphics. A short rule, applied again and again, makes a big pattern. The doorstep and the graphics card are running the same kind of program.

This kolam is built the way the mathematician Paulus Gerdes reconstructed Tamil designs: the line runs diagonally between the dots like a ray of light and bounces off small two-sided mirrors. With no mirrors, a 5×5 grid gives five separate lines. Tap the gaps between the dots to place mirrors, or press Join to let the rules do it.

“Never lift the hand” has a name in mathematics. In 1736 Leonhard Euler asked whether anyone could walk through the city of Königsberg crossing each of its seven bridges exactly once. Nobody could, and his proof of why started the field now called graph theory. The key was counting: a walk like that is only possible when the paths meet in even numbers.

A single-line kolam passes that test every time. At each crossing four arms meet, two in and two out, so one stroke can pass through them all and close where it began. The same question, can one route cover every path exactly once, is still how planners work out rounds for street sweepers, snowploughs and rubbish trucks.

So every morning, the doorstep runs a program written in rice flour. Lay out the grid. Follow the rules. Close the loop. Let the ants eat the output. Tomorrow, run it again.
