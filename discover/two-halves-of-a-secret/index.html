Then

A picture dyed in halves

In Patan, in north Gujarat, a patola sari (one piece is a patolu) begins long before the loom. Bundles of silk thread are tied tightly wherever a colour must not reach, dipped in dye, untied and tied again for the next colour. This is ikat. Most ikat dyes one set of threads. Patan patola dyes both: the warp that runs the length of the loom, and the weft that crosses it.

So the design is split in two before weaving begins. Tying and dyeing the threads for one six-yard sari takes three to four months, and a full piece can take months more on the loom. Each warp thread carries its part of the pattern; each weft thread carries the other part. Neither knows the picture on its own.

On the slanted wooden loom, weavers lay in each weft thread and nudge it into place with a slim rosewood stick called the vi, until every dyed spot meets its partner. Get it wrong and the motif blurs. Get it right, thread after thread, and parrots, elephants, flowers and diamond lattices come out crisp, and the same on both sides: a patola has no wrong side.

The Salvi weaving families of Patan have kept this craft alive for generations; tradition links their arrival to the royal patronage of Patan's medieval rulers. Patola travelled as far as Southeast Asia, was handed down as stridhan, a woman's own wealth, and Patan Patola received a Geographical Indication tag in 2013. Single-ikat patola, woven elsewhere in Gujarat, carries the design on the weft alone. Double ikat asks both halves to agree.

  • Step 1Tie

    Bind the silk wherever a colour must not reach.

  • Step 2Dye

    Dip, untie, re-tie, repeat for every colour, on warp and weft.

  • Step 3Align

    Weave, easing each thread into line with the vi.

  • Step 4Reveal

    The motif appears, the same on both sides.

Imagine

A secret in two layers

Now take the same idea and make it unbreakable. In 1994 two cryptographers, Moni Naor and Adi Shamir (the S in RSA), presented visual cryptography: a way to split a picture into two printed layers so that each one, on its own, is meaningless noise. Lay the two transparencies on top of each other, exactly in line, and the picture appears. No computer is needed to read it. Your eyes do the decoding.

The trick happens pixel by pixel. Each pixel becomes a tiny square of four dots on each layer. The first layer gets a random pattern. Where the picture is, the second layer copies that pattern; everywhere else, it gets the opposite. Stacked, picture pixels stay half lit and background pixels go dark.

Off

Here the two crafts part ways, in an interesting direction. A patola's warp alone still shows a smudged hint of the design; a careful eye could guess it. A visual-cryptography layer gives away nothing, because every pixel's pattern is chosen at random. Hold one layer up to the light all day: without its partner, there is nothing to find.

Naor and Shamir also showed how to share a picture among several people so that only enough of them together can see it. The same thinking, done with numbers instead of dots, is how important keys are guarded today: split a secret so that no single piece, and no single person, holds the whole thing.

The Salvi weavers would recognise the rule. A design that lives in two halves is only as good as their meeting. Line up every thread, every dot, and the picture comes out whole.
