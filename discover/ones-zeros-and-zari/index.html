Then

A pattern stored as yes and no

Walk down a lane in Varanasi and you'll hear it before you see it: the clack of a handloom. Look up, and above the weaver there's a long chain of stiff cards, folding over the top of the loom like a slow paper waterfall. Every card is full of holes. Together, they are the saree.

The pattern starts with a person, not a machine. The naqshaband is the designer behind every Banarasi. He draws the motif on graph paper, square by square: a paisley, a creeper, a little flowering buta. Each square is a decision. Thread up, or thread down.

Before punched cards, that drawing was turned into a naqsha, a frame of knotted cotton strings. The knots told the loom which warp threads to lift on each row, and a helper sat above the loom pulling the right cords at the right moment. The naqsha drawloom is thought to have reached India around a thousand years ago. Banaras was storing patterns as code long before anyone used the word.

In 1804, in Lyon, France, Joseph-Marie Jacquard replaced the helper with cards. Each card holds one row of the pattern. Where there's a hole, a needle passes through and that thread is lifted. Where there's no hole, the thread stays down. Lace the cards into a chain, and the loom weaves the pattern by itself. Change the cards, and the same loom weaves something new.

That idea travelled far beyond cloth. Around 1830, the English mathematician Charles Babbage borrowed punched cards for his design of a calculating machine, the Analytical Engine. Ada Lovelace, who wrote about it, put it beautifully: the engine "weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves."

Banaras weavers took up the Jacquard too. By one workshop's count, a single intricate Banarasi saree can need 1,500 to 2,500 punched cards. Today, many designs begin on a screen. DigiBunai, open-source design software from the government's Digital India Corporation, was first tried out with weavers in Varanasi in 2016. A designer can draw a motif on a computer, then print it for punching or send it straight to an electronic loom.

What stays the same is the idea underneath. A pattern is a long list of yes and no. Computers call that binary. Banaras calls it a saree.

Imagine

A blessing hidden in the pixels

Picture a grandmother's wedding saree, folded in a steel trunk in a Mumbai flat for forty years. Its gold border was woven from a chain of cards in Varanasi. The cards are long gone. The pattern is still there.

One afternoon, her granddaughter lays it flat on the bed and takes a photo. Nani watches, then asks a question. Can that photo carry a message? Something for the day her great-granddaughter wears it.

It can. A photo is a grid too, just like the naqshaband's graph paper, only the squares are far smaller. They're called pixels, and each one is stored as three numbers, for red, green and blue, each from 0 to 255.

Here's the trick. Change one of those numbers by one, say from 199 to 198, and no eye can see the difference. So the smallest yes or no in each number is spare room. Hiding a message there is called steganography, from the Greek for covered writing.

The idea is ancient. The Greek historian Herodotus tells of a messenger whose head was shaved and tattooed with a secret, then sent on his way once his hair had grown back. That's the difference between the two arts of secrets. Cryptography locks a message so no one can read it. Steganography hides it so no one knows it's there.

Take the letter M. Inside a computer, it's the number 77, which is 01001101 in binary. Give each of those eight yeses and noes to one pixel, in its smallest place. Six of the eight pixels already match. Only two numbers move, each by one. The saree looks exactly the same.

And there's plenty of room. A 12 megapixel phone photo has 36 million of these spare places. That's enough for about four and a half million letters, far more than any blessing needs.

What changed

There's one catch. Send the photo the usual way on WhatsApp, and the app squeezes it to save space. That squeezing scrambles those tiny numbers, and the message is lost. Send it as a document instead, and every pixel arrives untouched.

Now take the idea back to the loom. A weaver could do the same thing in thread: one extra thread lifted in every tenth repeat of the border, a change so small no one would notice unless they knew where to look. A date, a name, a blessing, woven into the zari itself. The card chain already speaks in yes and no. It could carry a second, quieter message too.

The same trick can protect the naqshaband. A hidden mark inside a digital design file, called a watermark, can carry his name wherever the file travels, even when someone copies it. Like any hiding place, it can hide bad things too, which is why security tools check images for hidden code.

Jacquard's cards hid a flower in a row of holes. A photo can hide a blessing in a row of pixels. It's the same idea, 200 years apart. A pattern is a long list of yes and no, and some of it can be meant for just one person.
