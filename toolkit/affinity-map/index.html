Affinity Map

Turn a pile of research notes into a few clear priorities, on one board with three columns. Work solo, or open a team board for groups of 4–5 (up to 30 people on one board).

Also called the KJ method, after its inventor, Japanese anthropologist Jiro Kawakita. The three steps follow Nielsen Norman Group. Where it comes from

#1
COLUMN 1 · 10 MIN

Generate

Write the research question at the top. Then everyone adds ideas on their own, without talking: one quote, fact or observation per note, between 1 and 30 notes. Paste a list if you have interview notes.

COLUMN 2 · 15 MIN

Organise

Type a cluster name and press Enter. Drag notes from Generate into clusters: each drag adds a copy, so the original stays in Generate with a tick. Name clusters with what you learned: “I plan my day around train timings”, not “Timing”.

COLUMN 3 · 10 MIN

Prioritise

Everyone picks up the dot tool in Organise and clicks the clusters that matter most. Each person on a team has five dots. Nothing to click in this column: it reads the dots and lines the clusters up in order, #1 at the top.

Where affinity mapping comes from

Affinity mapping is also called the KJ method, after its inventor, the Japanese anthropologist and ethnographer Jiro Kawakita. The name uses his initials, K. J., with the family name first, as in Japanese.

  • The inventor. Kawakita developed the technique in the 1960s while doing field research in Nepal.
  • The problem. He had a huge, messy pile of qualitative field notes and observations, and standard top-down, deductive Western methods couldn't make sense of them.
  • The solution. He worked bottom-up: notes are grouped by their natural “affinity”, meaning how closely related they are, before any labels or categories are put on them. Groups are named only after they have formed.

Because notes cluster by how similar they are, the practice became widely known as the affinity diagram or affinity mapping. It's one of the seven management and planning tools in quality management, and a standard step in UX research and design sprints, where teams use it to sort interview notes and observations.

The three steps

  1. Generate ideas as sticky notes, on your own first.
  2. Organise the notes into clusters and label them together.
  3. Prioritise the clusters with dot voting.

As described by Nielsen Norman Group (Rachel Krause and Kara Pernice, 2024).

Where your board is kept

Solo boards stay in your browser and are never uploaded. Team boards are stored on Google Firebase so teammates can see each other's notes. Anyone with the team code can view and edit that board, so don't put names or anything private on it.
